What replaced "dry"? Acidity, the quiet new pillar of modern Japanese sake

What 320,000 reviews and 10 years of search data reveal

Recap from the last piece

In my last data piece, "Is the era of dry sake over?", I arrived at this: 67% of SAKETIME's top 10 brands carry the "sweet" tag; only 4% are tagged "dry." In review text, "fruity" is the leading descriptor at 25%. And yet, on Google, "Japanese sake + dry" still outdraws "Japanese sake + sweet" by a factor of 2.5. My conclusion: 'dry' isn't dead — it's just stepped off the yardstick used to evaluate the top tier.

A comment that wouldn't leave me alone

When I cross-posted that piece to Instagram, one exchange in the comments stayed with me.

A user named @namazakepaul — Paul, who runs Namazake Paul Imports, a craft-sake importer in Portland, Oregon — left this comment: "The rise of acidity is interesting too." Paul's company describes its lineup as "unpasteurized, bold, high acid and sometimes cloudy craft sake" — that is, namazake (unpasteurized), bold, high in acid, occasionally hazy. An importer whose entire catalog is built around the very thing this article would eventually be about.

My reply ran something like: "Agreed — but acidity (sansa-mi) isn't an established sake vocabulary in the way karakuchi ('dry') is. SAKETIME's structured ratings only have sweet/dry and light/rich axes; there's no dedicated acidity scale, which limits the structural analysis. That said, the review text might have something to say. Let me dig, and report back."

Paul's reply lodged in my head for weeks:

That's interesting as Karakuchi isn't a regulated term but Total Acidity is an objective data point.

He had a point. Japanese sake does have a formal "acidity" indicator — measured by acid-base titration, printed on labels, and part of the National Tax Agency's standard chemical analysis of every commercial sake. The sweet/dry axis isn't purely subjective either: there's the sake meter value (nihonshu-do), a specific-gravity reading that broadly reflects residual sugar. I'm not going to claim "karakuchi is entirely subjective and only acidity is objective." But Paul's distinction is sharper than that. The term "karakuchi" itself isn't a regulated label — one brewery's "karakuchi" at sake meter +5 is another brewery's "medium-dry" at the very same number. There's no legal or industry consensus on where "karakuchi" begins. Acidity, by contrast, is the number — the titration result is printed directly on the bottle. "Karakuchi" is a name (a subjective translation of a number); "acidity" is the measurement itself. As far as label-level definition goes, the acidity side is the more clearly defined one. And yet, in review language, "acidity" looks far more marginal than "dry" or "sweet." Why does the side with the cleaner definition get talked about less? Or — if it has started to get talked about — when, how fast, and which kinds of acid?

The question I'd ended my last piece with — "if 'dry' stepped off the yardstick, what replaced it?" — and Paul's "the measured number, not the label name, is where acid actually lives" observation, finally connected in my head.

"Sweet" and "fruity" are the natural extensions of my previous conclusion. But anyone who's actually tasted the top brands — Aramasa, Senkin, Hinotori — knows they're not just sweet and fruity. There's another sensation that lifts on the tongue, common across them. It's acidity.

The acid-forward style that Aramasa and Senkin led. For most of sake history, "acid coming forward" was treated as close to a defect — the signature of hi-ochi spoilage, where bacterial action produces excess acetic acid. Somewhere along the way, that same rising acid stopped being a flaw and became a feature.

I wanted to see if this story is actually in the data. Using the same 320,000 reviews from my last piece, plus some new data and methods, I went back in. The conclusion, up front: acidity has quietly emerged over the last decade as the second protagonist of modern Japanese sake — partly inheriting the role 'dry' used to play. Paul, this is me finally making good on "I'll dig and report back."

1. Acidity, the sixth category

My last piece tracked five taste-keyword categories (Sweet, Dry, Rich, Light, Fruity) by decade. This time I added a sixth: Acidic.

Defining "acidic vocabulary" isn't trivial. I ended up with 16 words that chemically point to acidity — 酸味 (sansa-mi, "acid taste"), 酸度 (sando, "acidity reading"), 乳酸 ("lactic acid"), 甘酸っぱい/甘酸 ("sweet-and-sour"), citrus terms (柑橘・レモン・ライム・グレープフルーツ・柚子・シトラス), ヨーグルト (yogurt), ワイン・葡萄 (wine, grape), 梅干し・プラム (umeboshi, plum), ベリー (berry), キウイ (kiwi), and 白麹 (shiro-koji, white koji). Each is a word that, by itself, signals "this is about acidity" — high-precision, low-noise vocabulary only. (Methodology notes at the end.)

Fig. 1: Mention rates by year across all brands. The Acidic category climbed from 7.2% in 2014 to 27.5% in 2025 — a 3.82× rise.

The result was unambiguous.

In absolute change, Sweet led at +23.4 points; Acidic was second at +20.3 points. By multiplier, Sweet grew 4.58× and Acidic 3.82×. Acidic grew faster than Fruity, and more than twice as fast as Dry. This was a dimension I hadn't included in the last piece, and I'd been underestimating its scale.

Dry, meanwhile, grew the slowest of the six at 1.74×. My last piece's conclusion — "dry isn't dead" — holds up in this rerun. But sitting next to the other five, dry is being visibly left behind.

2. "Acid" is not one thing — it has four faces

You can't really see the story by staring at total acidic-vocabulary frequency. Japanese sake contains four major organic acids (see SakeStreet and SAKETIMES). Lactic, citric, malic, and tartaric. Each is produced through a different process and tastes different on the palate.

  • Lactic acid — round, creamy, yogurt-like. Develops naturally in kimoto and yamahai fermentation, traditional starter-mash methods that rely on wild lactic-acid bacteria.
  • Citric acid — sharp, bright, citrus-like. A signature of breweries adopting shiro-koji (white koji, Aspergillus luchuensis) or kuro-koji (black koji), molds originally used in awamori and shochu production.
  • Malic acid — clean, juicy, apple-like. Yeast-driven (Yeast No. 28 is the most famous example).
  • Tartaric acid — wine-like, often paired with grape and wine descriptors.

I split my 16 acidic-vocabulary words into these four buckets and tracked each year by year.

Fig. 2: Growth multipliers by acid type. Lactic-acid vocabulary rose from 0.12% in 2014 to 2.50% in 2026 — a 21× expansion that mirrors the kimoto/yamahai revival.

The most dramatic is lactic acid at 21×. The absolute mention rate is still modest, but the timing aligns perfectly with the renewed interest in kimoto and yamahai brewing. In my last piece I noted a small return of "dry" vocabulary after 2022; lactic-acid vocabulary was rising in the same window. What's coming back may not be "light dry" but "complex, savory dry."

Citric is the largest in absolute terms, reaching 3.87% in 2026. This tracks with the rise of brands adopting white koji (Fukucho, Kurokabuto, and others). As we'll see in §5, this curve almost exactly mirrors Google searches for "白麹" (white koji).

Tartaric vocabulary (grape, wine) was already at 1.49% in 2014 — the highest starting point of the four — and grew the least, at 1.8×. Wine-like descriptors have been part of the sake conversation for at least a decade. Not a new trend; an established axis.

3. The machine also surfaced an "acidity topic" — without being asked

So far this has been hypothesis-driven analysis — I decided which words count as "acidic." I also wanted to check the other direction: can a model find acidity as a topic on its own, with no input from me?

Topic models are statistical tools that find latent themes in document collections. You don't tell them what to look for; you ask "what are these documents about?" and let the math answer. I trained two models — LDA and DMR (a variant where year is included as a covariate, analogous to Structural Topic Models in R) — with K=20 topics on 247,485 preprocessed reviews.

The result: in LDA, 8 of 20 topics included at least one acid-related word (酸味, 酸, 酸度, キレ, シャープ, 爽やか, さっぱり, ジューシー, 乳酸) in their top 20 words. In DMR, the count was 9 of 20. LDA's Topic 2, for instance, clustered "carbonation · wine · gas · sparkling · refreshing · sparkling · sweet-and-sour · lactic · shuwa" — a clearly defined "acid-forward sparkling style" topic.

In other words: with no one prompting it about acidity, an unsupervised model looking at the corpus carves out acidity as one of its major themes. The vocabulary I chose isn't an artifact of my hypothesis — acidity functions as an independent dimension of evaluation, observable from outside my definition.

4. Four brands that mapped the territory of acid

Aramasa, Senkin, Tsuchida, Fukucho. I wanted to see whether each of these brands leans on a different one of the four acids, by computing the per-brand mention rate of my acidic vocabulary, split by acid type.

A note on brand selection: I picked these brands deliberately to represent each of the four acid types with at least one pioneer brand, plus two control brands known for clean, refined styles without a pronounced acidic identity. Specifically: Tsuchida for lactic (a yamahai/kimoto specialist), Fukucho and Hiran for citric (white-koji adopters), Aramasa and Senkin as the originators of the acid-forward style, plus Kazenomori (clean, slightly sparkling) and Jikon (delicate, refined high-rating brand) as controls. This is a curated set, not the top seven from a data-driven ranking; brands with fewer than 100 reviews were excluded.

A note on terminology: throughout this piece I use the brand name rather than the brewery name. In Japanese sake, the two are often different — Fukucho is made by Imada Shuzo, Kazenomori by Yucho Shuzo, Tsuchida by Tsuchida Shuzo (one of the cases where they coincide), and so on. Reviewers identify what they're drinking by brand, and the analysis here is brand-level (mention rates per brand). The terminology follows the data.

Fig. 3: Mention rate of each acid type by brand (full-period average, %). Each pioneer clusters around a different acid.

The standout numbers: Tsuchida's lactic at 13.1%, six times the all-brand average of 2.1%. And Fukucho's citric at 8.6%, 2.7× the average. Both line up perfectly with the producer's stated approach — Tsuchida Shuzo focuses on yamahai/kimoto, Imada Shuzo (the brewery behind Fukucho) leads with white koji in lines like Seafood (海風土).

Aramasa is interesting. Despite being one of the brands famously associated with the kimoto revival — a method that should produce lactic acid — its highest-scoring acids in the data are citric (8.5%) and tartaric (6.5%), with lactic actually lower at 3.8%. This reflects Aramasa's wide product range (No.6, PRIVATE LAB series, Hinotori, and more) — it's not a one-acid brand. "Kimoto brand = lactic-only" is too simple.

Senkin running above the global average on essentially every acid type reflects, I think, what you'd expect from a brand that's tried to express every facet of the acid-forward style. Jikon sits below average on every acid — also expected, given a brand whose stated aim is "delicate, refined" sake. In my last piece, Jikon appeared as the brand that refuses to commit to either sweet or dry. The pattern continues here, in the acid dimension.

5. Search behavior points the same way

So far this has all been about the language used in SAKETIME reviews. To check whether ordinary consumer search behavior aligns, I re-pulled Google Trends. I tracked three queries — 「日本酒 酸味」 ("Japanese sake acidity"), 「白麹」 ("white koji"), 「生酛」 ("kimoto") — from 2014 to 2026.

Fig. 4: Yearly average of the Google search index for three queries. "Kimoto" and "white koji" both rose 2–4× over the period; "sake acidity" itself never gained meaningful search volume.

Two things stand out.

First: consumers don't search for "acidity" — they search for the methods that produce it. Review writers (enthusiasts) write "acidity"; people who go on to buy a bottle search "white koji" and "kimoto." This gap probably reflects a literacy difference between the people writing about sake and the people buying it — the latter remember the technique name on the label, not the chemistry abstraction.

Second: Google Trends and review language are tightly correlated. Computing Pearson correlations on yearly data: white koji × citric vocabulary: r = 0.877. Kimoto × lactic vocabulary: r = 0.707. Both very high. The chemistry (white koji produces large amounts of citric acid), the brewing technique (more breweries adopting white koji), the search behavior (rising "white koji" searches), and the review language (rising citric vocabulary) all move together.

The "three worlds" I described in the last piece — top-of-ranking brands, lower-ranked brands, and general consumer search — pointed in different directions on the question of "dry." On the question of acidity, all three point the same way.

6. Rewriting the "three worlds"

The last piece ended with this table:

  • World 1 (top-ranked brands): 67% sweet, 4% dry — sweet, fruity, light
  • World 2 (lower-ranked brands): 28% sweet, 29% dry — split, "dry" still alive
  • World 3 (general consumer search): "sake dry" 2.5× "sake sweet"

After this round of analysis, here's the parallel "three worlds of acidity":

  • World 1 (top-10 ranked): CORE acidic vocabulary at 20.8%; CORE ∪ RELATED exceeds 30%. Top brands are unambiguously talked about in acidic terms.
  • World 2 (lower-ranked): not very different from the global average. Acidic vocabulary is rising across the whole distribution, not just at the top.
  • World 3 (general consumer search): "acidity" itself isn't searched, but "white koji" and "kimoto" are 2–4× higher than 2014. Interest reaches the general audience, translated into method names.

"Dry" was the axis that was kept by the lower half and the searches, but discarded at the top. Acidity is the opposite — it's rising at every layer. That's a phenomenon that wasn't observable ten years ago.

Conclusion: the center didn't just move — it fragmented

The last piece concluded: "the center has moved from dry to sweet/fruity — but dry hasn't been left entirely behind." What I'd add here, as a continuation: the identity of the new center is more complicated than that.

"Sweet" and "fruity" don't fully capture what makes modern top brands what they are. Aramasa's kimoto, Senkin's malic profile, Tsuchida's yamahai, Fukucho's white koji — four brands all described as "acid-forward sake" — are producing four different acids via four different chemistries, and leaving four different patterns in the review language. The differences between Aramasa and Senkin, between Tsuchida and Fukucho, only become legible once you split the data into four buckets.

And consumers don't search for "acidity." They do search — increasingly — for "white koji" and "kimoto." Interest in acidity is reaching the general public, but it arrives translated from a taste term into a method term.

The "dry" era was a one-dimensional yardstick era — tanrei-karakuchi, "light and dry." The next era is one where four different chemistries tell four different stories, and consumers learn them by the names of the brewing methods. The language of sake is only just starting to catch up with the complexity it's producing.

What came after "dry" is acidity. That's the view from my data. But if you write it off as "acidity" in a single word, you lose half the picture. The roundness of lactic, the sharpness of citric, the juiciness of malic, the wine-like complexity of tartaric. If you have a bottle in front of you tonight, try asking which of the four it is. It will probably read differently than the last time you drank it.

Data and methods

Primary data - SAKETIME reviews: 367,330 reviews across 4,737 brands, 2014–2026. After filtering for non-empty comments with parseable dates, the working corpus was 322,644 reviews. - Japanese morphological analysis: fugashi + unidic-lite. Verbs were excluded (low POS accuracy on review text); analysis used nouns, adjectives, and na-adjectives only. Single-character tokens, brand names, and standard stopwords were removed. - 白麹 (white koji) is split by the tokenizer into "white + koji." A user-dictionary-equivalent post-processing step reconstitutes it as a single token. - Google Trends: pytrends rate-limiting forced a hybrid of API pulls and manual CSV downloads.

ACIDITY_CORE and ACIDITY_RELATED - CORE (4 words): 酸味, 酸っぱい, 酸度, 乳酸. Words that, alone, can only mean acidity. - RELATED (16 words): curated based on chemistry. Citrus terms (citric acid), yogurt (lactic), grape/wine (tartaric), umeboshi/plum/berry/kiwi (malic-family fruits), "sweet-and-sour" expressions, and the method name 白麹 (white koji).

As an earlier step, I also ran a Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) analysis to mechanically extract words that statistically co-occur with the CORE set. The top hits included generic taste/structure words (sweetness, bitterness, astringency, finish, attack, balance, etc.). Useful for context, but not specifically "acidic vocabulary" — these are words that appear alongside acidity descriptors, not synonyms for them. They were excluded from the RELATED set used for trend measurement.

Topic models - LDA: tomotopy.LDAModel, K=20, min_cf=50, rm_top=20, iter=500. - DMR (Dirichlet-Multinomial Regression LDA): year as a covariate, used to model topic distribution change directly as a function of year. Roughly the Python equivalent of R's stm (Structural Topic Models).

Code and reproducibility: the analysis notebook and figure-generation script live in a private GitHub repository. All figures in this piece were produced from analysis/acidity_topic_analysis.ipynb.
